The Hypothesis

The market has a weekly rhythm.
Can you exploit it?

The Day-of-Week (DoW) Effect is one of the most studied calendar anomalies in finance. The core idea: stock returns are not uniformly distributed across the five trading days. Some days systematically deliver lower returns than others — and for a long-term SIP investor, consistently buying on a "cheaper" day could compound meaningfully over decades.

📉

The Monday Effect

Documented since 1973, US markets historically deliver negative returns on Mondays. Bad news accumulates over weekends and gets priced in at Monday's open — creating a consistent downward drift.

🌏

The Asian Tuesday Shift

In Japan, India and other Asian markets, the negative effect shifts to Tuesday. Monday's US close transmits to Asian Tuesday opens via overnight futures. India absorbs global weekend sentiment a full day later.

📈

The Friday Strength

Fridays tend to be strong globally. Institutional traders square positions, retail sentiment is positive pre-weekend, and short-sellers cover — creating an upward bias. The worst day to SIP.

🔑 Key finding from our data

Across 4.5 years of daily data, Wednesday is the single most consistent best SIP day for Indian markets (Nifty 50: +27.89% cumulative Wednesday-only returns vs –10.30% Thursday). For US markets, Monday dominates (S&P 500: +25.20% Monday vs –9.71% Thursday). For Japan (Nikkei), Tuesday is the clear winner (+62.97% cumulative). Friday is the worst SIP day in 9 out of 13 markets studied.

Academic Research

50 years of evidence behind
the day-of-week effect

This isn't a new discovery. The Day-of-Week effect is one of the most replicated and debated findings in financial economics. Here's the complete intellectual history.

1973
Frank Cross — The first observation
In his landmark paper "The Behavior of Stock Prices on Fridays and Mondays" in the Financial Analysts Journal, Cross documented that S&P 500 returns from 1953–1970 were positive on Fridays 62% of the time but only 39.5% of the time on Mondays. The Weekend Effect was born. This was the first quantitative proof that weekday isn't just a label — it statistically predicts return direction.
1980
Kenneth French — The Monday Effect formalised
"Stock Returns and the Weekend Effect" in the Journal of Financial Economics is the most-cited paper in this space. French found that S&P 500 returns from 1953–1977 were significantly negative on Mondays (–0.17% on average) while all other days were positive. He proposed the "trading time" vs "calendar time" hypothesis: if returns were uniform, overnight and weekend returns should equal trading session returns. They don't. The paper also posed the key puzzle: if everyone knows about this, why hasn't it been arbitraged away?
1984
Gibbons & Hess — It's the weekend, not just Monday
Gibbons and Hess showed that the negative Monday return actually originates during the weekend — not during Monday trading hours. Stocks gap down at the Monday open, and the average intraday return on Monday is actually slightly positive. The implication: bad news released Friday after-hours or over the weekend gets fully priced in at Monday's open. This makes the SIP timing question about the open price, not just the daily return.
1985
Jaffe & Westerfield — The Asian Tuesday Effect
This is the most relevant paper for Indian investors. Jaffe and Westerfield studied Australia, Canada, Japan, and the UK. They found that Australian and Japanese markets had their lowest returns on Tuesdays, not Mondays. The mechanism: these markets trade on Tuesday when the US market's Monday session has already closed. US Monday's negative close transmits to Tokyo and Sydney Tuesday opens via overnight futures. India, being in a similar timezone relationship with the US, shows the same Tuesday spillover effect — precisely what we see in the Nifty data.
1990s
International confirmation wave
Multiple studies confirmed the DoW effect globally: Solnik & Bousquet (1990) found negative Tuesday returns in France; Athanassakos & Robinson (1994) in Canada; Board & Sutcliffe (1988) in the UK; Ho (1990) across 8 Asian markets. Each market had its own "weak" day depending on its relationship with the US close. This cross-market transmission mechanism became the dominant explanation — and it's precisely why India's "bad day" is Tuesday rather than Monday.
2000–2005
The anomaly begins to weaken in developed markets
Mehdian & Perry (2001) showed the Monday effect in the US had reversed post-1987 — US large-caps actually earned positive Monday returns in the 1990s. Steeley (2001) found the UK Monday effect disappeared post-1990. Interpretation: as institutional awareness grew and algorithmic arbitrage became possible, the easy money was taken. But emerging markets like India lagged — their anomalies persist longer because information dissemination is slower and transaction costs are higher. This is a classic story of market efficiency catching up gradually.
2005–2015
Indian market studies emerge
Several papers specifically studied Indian markets: Patel (2008) studied BSE Sensex returns from 1996–2006 and found significant Tuesday weakness. Raj & Kumari (2006) confirmed the negative Tuesday effect in Indian markets across multiple indices. Kaur (2010) found the effect strongest in mid-caps and small-caps. The consensus: India has a clearly identifiable Tuesday weakness that persists across market cycles, though the magnitude varies year to year.
2015–Present
The SIP timing question enters the discourse
As SIP investing became mainstream in India (AMFI data shows SIP book growing from ₹4,000 Cr/month in 2016 to ₹26,000+ Cr/month by 2025), researchers began quantifying whether SIP timing adds value. Multiple studies (including analysis by AMFI-registered distributors and academics) found that while the day effect is statistically significant over long periods, consistency of investing matters 10x more than the day chosen. Still, for a cost-free optimisation, Tuesday or Wednesday remains the most defensible choice for Indian SIP investors.
The Mechanisms

Why does this effect exist?

📰
Weekend news accumulation
Companies, governments, and central banks release bad news Friday afternoons and over weekends when markets are closed — minimising immediate reaction. This negative sentiment gets digested at Monday's (or Tuesday's) open, creating systematic downward pressure at week-start.
🌐
Cross-market contagion
US market movements ripple to Asian markets the next session via futures and ADR pricing. When the S&P 500 falls on Monday, Nifty futures reflect it Tuesday morning. India's Tuesday weakness is partly borrowed from America's Monday weakness — a time-zone transmission effect.
🏦
Institutional settlement patterns
Institutional investors — mutual funds, insurance companies, FIIs — have weekly settlement and portfolio rebalancing rhythms. They tend to be sellers at week-start (Monday/Tuesday) as they process weekend risk reviews, and buyers toward week-end (Thursday/Friday) to deploy fresh allocations.
😊
Investor psychology & mood
Behavioural finance research (Hirshleifer & Shumway, 2003) links sunshine and good weather to positive returns. The Monday Blues — the documented psychological phenomenon where people feel worse on Mondays — also affects trading sentiment. Retail investors in particular tend to be net sellers on Mondays.
📋
Options expiry & derivatives
In India, weekly Nifty options expire on Thursdays. This creates structured buying/selling pressure as market makers and option writers hedge positions mid-week (Tuesday-Wednesday hedging selloffs) and unwind by Thursday close, contributing to the Wednesday dip pattern we observe.
🤖
Why it doesn't disappear
If the effect is so well known, why hasn't arbitrage eliminated it? Because the edge (0.1–0.2% per day) is too small to overcome transaction costs for active traders. But for a buy-and-hold SIP investor who pays zero marginal cost to choose Tuesday over Friday, the edge is genuinely free — and that's why it persists.
SIP Optimisation

The practical guide for
Indian SIP investors

Here's what our data, plus 50 years of academic research, actually tells you to do — and not to do.

✅ What the data says to do
  • → Set your SIP date to Tuesday or Wednesday for Indian indices and mutual funds. Our data shows Wednesday has the lowest cumulative return across 4.5 years (meaning cheaper average price paid over time).
  • → Prioritise consistency over timing. An SIP missed because you were waiting for "the right day" destroys far more value than any day-of-week optimisation can provide.
  • → For US equity funds (like Parag Parikh Flexicap) which have significant US exposure, Wednesday still wins in our data (+26.07% cumulative vs –1.46% Friday over all data).
  • → Think in decades, not days. The day effect is a 0.1–0.2% weekly edge. Compounded over 20 years of SIP, it's meaningful — but it requires staying invested through market cycles, not timing exits.
❌ What NOT to do
  • ✗ Don't set SIP on Friday — it's the most expensive day in 9 out of 13 markets, and the worst by cumulative return for Indian indices over 1Y and 3Y periods.
  • ✗ Don't try to time the market week by week based on this data. The effect is a statistical average — any given Tuesday can be a +2% day. It only works compounded over hundreds of SIP installments.
  • ✗ Don't ignore your fund's global exposure. NASDAQ and FTSE data in our set is limited (<30 observations) — don't over-read those patterns.
  • ✗ Don't let this analysis cause you to stop or pause your SIP. The biggest risk to your SIP return isn't which day you invest — it's stopping during a correction.

Methodology: All returns are computed as close-to-close percentage change (the "Change %" column from Investing.com daily OHLCV data). Returns are grouped by calendar day of week (Monday=0 through Friday=4). Analysis spans January 2022 – June 2026 for most markets (~4.5 years, ~220 observations per weekday). NASDAQ, FTSE Europe, Gold, Silver, Crude Oil, Nifty 100, and Nifty 500 data covers only the most recent ~1 month (~4–5 observations per day) — results for these should be treated as indicative, not statistically robust. Cumulative returns are computed as compounded product of all daily returns on that weekday: ∏(1 + r_i/100) − 1.

Note on sample sizes: Markets with ≥200 observations per weekday (Nifty 50, S&P 500, Dow Jones, Shanghai, Nikkei 225, Parag Parikh MF) are statistically robust. Markets with <10 observations per weekday should be treated as directional indicators only.
