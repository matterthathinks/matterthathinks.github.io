Manufacturing share of GDP surges to 30%+. 02 ## The Foundational Argument: State Capacity Most comparisons focus on what India and China chose to do differently. The deeper question is what each country was *capable* of doing. State capacity — the ability to design, fund, and enforce policy at scale — is the precondition that made China's programmes possible. It is where the real divergence begins. "India in 1947 lacked the state capacity to run a mass literacy campaign. China had a single-party apparatus willing to coerce compliance. The real comparison is not just policy preference — it is state capacity as a precondition for policy execution." China's mechanism Political incentives, not ideology What drove China's mass education was not communist ideology alone — it was **fiscal decentralization combined with political accountability**. Under Mao, local party officials' careers literally depended on literacy metrics. Under Deng, the 1986 Compulsory Education Law made local governments financially responsible for 9-year schooling. The incentive structure aligned bureaucratic ambition with education outcomes. India's constraint Democracy and state fragmentation India's federal, democratic structure — a genuine strength in many ways — made large-scale coercive mobilisation impossible. Centre-state tensions, political competition, and patronage networks meant education policy was inconsistently enforced. The Right to Education Act came in **2009** — 60 years after China's initial literacy drives. Near-universal enrollment was achieved only around 2000, where China reached it by the mid-1980s. The lesson Execution, not just design India designed good policies — Sarva Shiksha Abhiyan, mid-day meal schemes, RTE. The failure was **delivery**. Teacher absenteeism, infrastructure gaps, and urban-rural quality divides meant enrollment didn't translate to learning. ASER reports consistently show that 50%+ of Class V students cannot read a Class II text. The problem is not intent — it is the pipeline from policy to classroom. China's enforcement The 1986 Compulsory Education Law After Deng's 1978 reforms, China enacted the **Compulsory Education Law in 1986**, mandating 9 years of schooling for all children and giving local governments financial responsibility. This combined national mandate with local accountability. Central government could monitor, local officials were responsible, parents had legal standing. The mechanism worked. India's trajectory Late, uneven progress India's primary enrollment rate in 1950 was \~43%. China's was similar — but China made universal basic education a state mobilization goal from 1949. By 1978, when China opened its economy, it had a **literate, trainable workforce** ready to staff factories. India was still catching up in basic literacy when its own economic opening arrived in 1991. The workforce wasn't ready the same way. The nuance Neither model is simply "better" China's authoritarian capacity enabled rapid execution but also produced disasters (Great Leap Forward, Cultural Revolution). India's democracy is slower but more resilient and corrective. The question is whether India can find **democratic mechanisms for high-execution governance** — as South Korea and Taiwan eventually did within their own political trajectories. **The sharpest question:** Was India's choice really a choice, or a constraint? India in 1947 lacked the state capacity to run a mass literacy campaign. China had a single-party apparatus. The real comparison is not just policy preference — it's state capacity as a precondition for policy execution. Critiquing India's choices without acknowledging this asymmetry is unfair. Ignoring it is also misleading. 03 ## Human Capital: The 1950 Fork The standard narrative places the India-China divergence at 1978 (Deng's reforms) or 1991 (India's liberalisation). David Oks's argument is more uncomfortable: the decisive moment was **1950** — when both governments attempted sweeping family law reform, and only one succeeded. "The moment of divergence, I think, came not in 1978, or in 1991, but around 1950 — when China passed the New Marriage Law, while India failed to pass the Hindu Code Bill." \ — David Oks, *Why China Got Rich and India Didn't* (2026) Rapid industrialisation requires workers who are literate enough to be trained, healthy enough to show up, disciplined enough to come in on time, and sufficiently free of traditional obligations that they can sell their labour to whoever offers the most for it. Traditional agrarian societies produce almost none of these people. China spent three decades — under Mao, brutally — creating them. India's democratic state never did. ### The fork: two laws, two outcomes **China: New Marriage Law (1950) — Passed**\ \ Banned arranged marriage, concubinage, and child betrothal. Women gained the right to own property and divorce freely. The CCP enforced compliance — local cadres' careers depended on it. Traditional patriarchal authority was systematically dismantled. Women were mobilised under "women hold up half the sky." Tens of millions entered economic life for the first time. 1950 New Marriage Law passed by fiat Single-party apparatus enabled immediate national enforcement. Resistance branded as class disloyalty. 1950s–70s Mass female mobilisation Female literacy rises from near-zero to \~50% by 1982. China's female LFPR reaches \~61% — exceeding many rich countries. Child mortality falls 80% between early 1950s and late 1970s. Life expectancy: 41 years (1949) → 61 years (1976). 1978 Economic opening — workforce ready When Deng opened China's economy, a literate, healthy, fully mobilised workforce — including women — was already waiting. The precondition had been built over 28 years. **India: Hindu Code Bill (1950) — Failed**\ \ Nehru and Ambedkar introduced sweeping reform — ban polygamy, give women the right to divorce and inherit property. Congress was not united. Opposition from Hindu traditionalists *within Congress itself* proved decisive. Ambedkar resigned in disgust. Watered-down reforms passed with major concessions. Agricultural land — the majority of useful property — was exempted from inheritance reform. 1951 Bill dies in parliament; Ambedkar resigns India's president attacked the bill. Democratic consensus-building failed at the first test of transformative social reform. 1950s–70s Customs persist; enforcement absent Watered-down reforms passed but unenforced at the local level. Female literacy in 1981: only 26%. Female LFPR today: \~27% — one of the world's lowest, closer to Afghanistan than China. 1991 Economic opening — workforce unprepared India liberalised 13 years after China with a workforce that was less literate, less healthy, and with far fewer women in it. The same global market, but only half the available labour force. --- ### The workforce consequence Human Capital Comparison — China 1950–1980 vs India 1950–1980 China \~65% Literacy rate (1982); up from 20% in 1949 India \~43% Literacy rate (1981); female at 26% China LFPR (late 1970s) \~61% Female labour force participation; exceeds many rich countries India LFPR (late 2010s) \~27% Female labour force participation; one of world's lowest China labour force (2019 vs India) 45% larger Despite India having a larger population Median income growth 1987–2022 88% India vs China's 611% — the compounded consequence Sources: David Oks, *Why China Got Rich and India Didn't* (Substack, 2026); World Bank WDI; UNESCO UIS. **The precondition argument:** Before asking why India didn't get the manufacturing boom, ask whether it had the workforce for one. This is not a cultural critique. It is a structural accounting of what social modernisation does for an economy, and what its absence costs. 04 ## Deng's Beehive: Incentives & Execution The standard explanation — "China is authoritarian so it can build things" — is wrong, or at least incomplete. There are plenty of authoritarian states that built nothing. The real mechanism is more interesting: Deng Xiaoping changed what millions of local officials were *rewarded for*. "What Deng does is change the way officials are graded — what they're judged by, how they move up. Every government official in China is literally graded, with a grade sheet. At the top, in big letters: economic development." \ — Bruce Dickson, George Washington University, via Johnny Harris (2024) ### The five-step incentive machine Step 1 · 1978–1984 Deng changes the scorecard Local officials evaluated primarily on economic development — GDP growth, tax revenue, investment attracted. Ideology retreats; technical competence and growth metrics advance. Millions of ambitious officials now have a clear path to promotion: make your region's economy grow. This alignment of bureaucratic self-interest with development outcomes is the core mechanism. Step 2 · 1980s "Eating in separate kitchens" — fiscal decentralisation Previously, all provincial revenue went to Beijing ("eating out of one big pot"). Under Deng's reform, local governments negotiate a fixed payment to the centre and **keep everything above it**. Now it's not just officials who benefit from growth — it's governments themselves, with real money to reinvest. The act of growing the local economy becomes directly profitable for the local state. Step 3 · 1980s–90s Township and Village Enterprises (TVEs) Local officials, armed with fiscal incentives and a clear GDP scorecard, start factories. Town governments own and run enterprises, collect the revenue, reinvest. Farmers become factory workers without leaving the countryside — "leaving the land, but not the countryside." Rural areas industrialise bottom-up, not top-down. TVEs account for a third of China's industrial output by the mid-1990s. Step 4 · 1994 onwards Land financing — the infrastructure engine After a fiscal recentralisation reduced local tax retention, governments found a new engine: land. Rural collective land is requisitioned at low cost, infrastructure is built on it (raising its value), and use-rights are leased to developers at a premium. Governments borrow against rising land value. The act of building itself becomes a revenue source — and building raises GDP, which raises officials' scores. Step 5 · Continuous Inter-jurisdictional competition Local officials compete with each other for investment, talent, and central approval. A nationwide race to build: roads, ports, rail, universities, industrial parks. National projects (Three Gorges Dam, high-speed rail) are politically possible because every jurisdiction along the route also benefits from being on the scorecard. Competition built China's infrastructure; coordination built its scale. --- ### Why India couldn't replicate this China's accountability chain GDP scorecards → career advancement An official who grew his district's economy was promoted. Measurable, verifiable, hard targets. Land reform and labour mobilisation could be enforced because resistance was politically costly for the resistors, not the reformers. India's political reality Coalition management → career advancement A district collector's career advanced through navigating caste coalitions, patronage networks, and seniority. Land reform failed because landlords had political representatives. Labour law reform failed because unions had political representatives. Every disrupted constituency had democratic voice. The honest framing Not authoritarianism — incentive alignment The question is not whether India should have been more authoritarian. It is whether its democratic federal structure had *any mechanism* to align official incentives with development outcomes. It largely didn't. Finding that mechanism — democratic but high-execution — is India's unfinished governance challenge. **The two scorecard items:** Under Deng's system, officials were evaluated on (1) economic development/GDP — the "hard target" (fail this and you are fired), and (2) social stability — the "veto target" (trigger a major protest and you are fired regardless of GDP). This dual structure explains why China's officials simultaneously pursued aggressive growth *and* maintained tight control over political dissent. Growth was the path to promotion; instability was the path to dismissal. The beehive produced both factories and surveillance. 05 ## Programmes, Policies and Deliberate Bets From the 1950s onward, China made a series of sequenced, compounding investments in science, technology, and industry. India made some good bets (IITs, ISRO, DAE) but missed the connective tissue between them — the programmes that link research to commercialisation, and education to employment. China · 1956 12-Year Science & Technology Plan The first strategic S&T roadmap - China's first national S&T plan identified 57 key tasks including atomic energy, electronics, and jet propulsion — 8 years before India had an equivalent framework. - Crucially, it framed scientific development as a *state security and development priority*, not merely an academic exercise. - India had the 1958 Science Policy Resolution (Nehru) but lacked comparable institutional follow-through and execution machinery. China · March 1986 Programme 863 — State High-Tech R&D Plan Seven fields, one letter, a transformed nation - Four scientists wrote to Deng Xiaoping warning that China was falling behind the global tech revolution. Deng approved the programme within 48 hours. Named for its date: March (3rd month) 1986. - Targeted 7 fields: biology, aerospace, information technology, lasers, automation, energy, new materials. Marine technology added 1996. Initial fund: 10 billion RMB — 5% of all government spending that year. - Inspired by Reagan's Strategic Defense Initiative. Over 20 years: 120,000+ research papers, 8,000+ patents, 1,800+ national industry standards. - India had no equivalent systematic high-tech R&D programme at this scale in this period. China · August 1988 Programme TORCH — Industrialisation & Incubation The programme that built China's startup infrastructure - Approved by State Council to bridge lab and market: created Science and Technology Industrial Parks (STIPs), Technology Business Incubators (TBIs), Innofund seed capital, and a Venture Guiding Fund. - The first incubator was built in Wuhan (1987), Shanghai followed (1988). The idea: *artificially create clustering* by concentrating resources, talent, and capital to a critical threshold. - Result: China now has 11,000+ incubators and accelerators. India has \~950 (2023). India's Startup India programme — the closest equivalent — came in **2016, 28 years later**. - Israel, Singapore and China are cited as the only three countries to successfully create innovation clusters by deliberate government design. China · 1995 / 1998 Project 211 & Project 985 — World-Class Universities Building the top of the pyramid, deliberately - **Project 211 (1995)**: Named for the 21st century and 100 institutions. Made research universities compete for major government grants, forcing them to build faculty, labs, and infrastructure. 112 universities ultimately included. - **Project 985 (1998)**: Jiang Zemin declared China must have "world-class universities." Started with 9 (C9 League = China's Ivy League), expanded to 39. Billions poured into faculty, international hiring, and research infrastructure. - Then **Double First Class (2015–17)**: 140 universities targeted to become world-class by 2050. China overtook the USA in Nature Index natural-sciences output in 2022. - Crucially: China did this *while maintaining mass primary education*. India built IITs but without the same simultaneous investment in the layer beneath them. China · 2015 Made in China 2025 Industrial upgrading for the next century - Targeted 10 strategic sectors: AI, electric vehicles, semiconductors, biotechnology, aerospace, advanced materials, robotics, maritime engineering, rail equipment, agricultural machinery. - Explicit goal: move from "factory of the world" to "innovator for the world." Reduce dependence on foreign technology in critical sectors. - India's equivalent — National Manufacturing Policy (2011), PLI schemes (2020+) — is more recent and less integrated with a broader innovation ecosystem. India · 1951–2016 India's IIT / IIM / AIIMS programme Building the top — without the full pyramid - India's strategy was elite-first: IIT Kharagpur (1951), IIM Calcutta (1961), AIIMS Delhi (1956). By 2000: only 7 IITs, 6 IIMs, 1 AIIMS — a deliberately scarce tier. - These institutions created India's global tech reputation, IT sector, and diaspora presence. They were a *good bet* — not the problem. - The failure: India built islands of excellence without simultaneously building the vocational layer, the research-to-market pipeline, or the primary education quality beneath them. - Nehru called IIT Kharagpur "a symbol of India, a fine monument of what free India is building." The trickle-down theory of human capital — that elite engineers would lift all boats — was the implicit, ultimately incomplete, assumption. **What India's programme list is missing:** A TORCH equivalent (incubators, STIPs, commercialisation pipeline). A Project 211 equivalent (systematic funding of 100+ universities). A vocational education strategy tied to industrial demand. These are not gaps in elite education — they are gaps in the *ecosystem around* elite education. 06 ## Education & Human Capital: The Full Pyramid Development economists think of human capital as a pyramid: a broad base of basic literacy, a middle tier of vocational and technical skills, and a narrow top of elite researchers and innovators. But human capital needs an industrial ecosystem to deploy into. China built that ecosystem deliberately — SEZs, export policy, supply chain integration. India didn't. The question of why India became service-led (rather than manufacturing-led) is not just about education. It is about industrial policy. "Manufacturing success requires basic education, industrial policy, and state-led coordination — not just one of the three. China did all three. India did one." ### China's SEZ model 1979 First Special Economic Zones Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Shantou, Xiamen. Land, labour, and tax advantages concentrated in specific geographies. Export-oriented manufacturing given preferential treatment. Shenzhen had a population of 30,000 in 1979; today it has 17 million and houses global electronics supply chains. 1984 14 coastal cities opened Foreign investment actively courted. Joint ventures required technology transfer. China was simultaneously building market access and absorbing foreign expertise. 2001 WTO Accession China's entry into the WTO at this moment — after two decades of SEZ experience, workforce training, and export infrastructure — was decisive. India's WTO integration was more gradual and without the same manufacturing base to leverage it. 2010s Moving up the value chain From low-cost assembly to high-tech manufacturing. Huawei, BYD, DJI, CATL emerge. China begins competing in sectors previously dominated only by USA, Japan, and Germany. **Why SEZs worked in China but struggle in India:**\ \ China's SEZs worked because of the full stack: cheap literate labour (mass education), political will to acquire land and relocate people, state-built infrastructure, and global supply chain relationships built over decades. India's SEZs, introduced in 2005, faced land acquisition disputes, regulatory complexity, and a workforce not trained for factory-floor roles at scale. The instrument was copied; the preconditions were not. **Vietnam's lesson (very relevant):** Vietnam entered global manufacturing supply chains by offering what China could no longer offer cheaply — low costs, political stability, and a compliant, basic-literate workforce. India has the population but not the *configured* workforce. Vietnam optimized for factories; India optimized for software engineers. Both are valid choices, but India's choice has lower employment multipliers. --- ### Why India became service-led — and whether that was a choice India did not consciously decide to become a services economy. It became one because that is where its inherited advantages (English, IITs, mathematics) met global demand (Y2K, outsourcing wave). After 1991 liberalisation, services had lower barriers to entry than manufacturing in India's regulatory environment. The path of least resistance led to Bengaluru, not to a factory floor. India's accidental strength The IT sector boom The Y2K crisis and subsequent outsourcing wave created demand that exactly matched India's supply of English-speaking, technically trained graduates. Without planning it, India became the world's back office. This is worth \~$250B in IT-BPO exports today. A genuine success — but with low employment multipliers relative to manufacturing. The structural trap Services don't absorb mass labour India adds \~12 million people to its working-age population every year. IT services employs 5 million total after 30 years of growth. Manufacturing, by contrast, can absorb semi-skilled workers at scale — as China demonstrated by employing 150+ million factory workers by the 2000s. India's growth model is structurally unable to absorb its demographic dividend. China's counterfactual lesson Both is possible China built mass manufacturing *and* elite universities (Projects 211 and 985). The dichotomy between "elite education" and "mass manufacturing" is false — it requires the right sequencing and institutional capacity to pursue both simultaneously. South Korea and Taiwan also demonstrate this: strong manufacturing and strong universities, built in parallel. The gap is 11:1 in incubators; the gap in deep-tech startup output is even wider. 08 ## Infrastructure, Investment & State Coordination Even with the right education and industrial policy, manufacturing requires physical infrastructure — ports, roads, power, and logistics. China invested at a scale and speed that India has not matched. Investment rates, infrastructure quality, and the state's ability to coordinate land, labour, and capital are the final piece of the puzzle. \~50% China's investment rate % of GDP (peak years) \~30% India's investment rate % of GDP $496B China R&D spend 2024 2.58% of GDP $23B India R&D spend 2024 0.65% of GDP ### What China built (1980–2010) Ports & Logistics - 7 of world's 10 busiest ports are Chinese - Container throughput 10× India's - Inland logistics networks linked SEZs to ports - Average container dwell time 1–2 days Power & Energy - Total installed capacity: 3,348 GW - India: 453 GW (7× smaller) - Reliable 24/7 industrial power essential for manufacturing competitiveness Land & SEZ Execution - Single-party state enables land acquisition at scale - Industrial parks built in 12–18 months - India: land acquisition legally contested, multi-year delays Rail & Roads - High-speed rail: 42,000 km (world's largest) - National expressway: 177,000 km - India: 99 km HSR (Ahmedabad-Mumbai, in progress) ### India's constraints Land acquisition The single biggest bottleneck India's Land Acquisition Act requires consent from affected communities, environmental impact assessments, and compensation at market rates. Legitimate democratic safeguards — but they make building industrial corridors and SEZs far slower than China's model. Labour laws Complex regulation → small firms India's labour laws historically encouraged firms to stay small (under 100 workers) to avoid compliance requirements. Result: 80%+ of workforce in informal economy, no large-scale factory employment. Firms prefer Vietnam (and increasingly Bangladesh) for scalable low-cost manufacturing. Power reliability 24/7 industrial power only recently achieved Reliable electricity is a prerequisite for competitive manufacturing. India's power infrastructure has improved dramatically but still has regional gaps. Manufacturing firms bear higher backup generator costs than Chinese counterparts. PLI schemes (2020+) India's belated industrial policy response Production-Linked Incentive schemes across 14 sectors are India's most serious industrial policy in decades. Early results are mixed — some sectors (mobile phones, APIs) are working; others face approval delays. The direction is right; execution is still being tested. **The investment rate gap matters enormously:** China invests \~50% of GDP; India \~30%. This difference — compounded over 40 years — explains physical infrastructure gaps more than any single policy decision. Manufacturing requires upfront capital (factories, logistics, power). Countries that invest more simply build more. India's investment rate needs to rise significantly and be directed toward the right sectors. 09 ## What India Missed — and What It Can Still Do This is not a story of India failing. India's IT sector, pharmaceutical industry, space programme, and democratic institutions are genuine achievements. But the honest accounting of what was missed — and what can still be built — is important. The demographic window is still open. "India didn't fail because it built IITs. It failed to build the rest of the system around them." Missed · 1960s–1990s Mass primary education quality Enrollment expanded but quality did not keep pace. No equivalent of China's political accountability mechanism for learning outcomes. ASER reports document the learning crisis 60 years later. The consequence: a workforce that enrolled in school but did not acquire factory-floor or technical skills. Missed · 1980s–2010s Vocational ecosystem at scale India's ITIs exist but were never tied to industry demand the way China's vocational schools were to its factories. Only \~2% of upper secondary students in vocational tracks vs 25% in China. The missing middle: tens of millions of technicians, CNC operators, quality inspectors, and logistics coordinators that a manufacturing economy needs. Missed · 1988–2016 A TORCH equivalent No national programme to systematically build incubators, science parks, and lab-to-market pipelines until Startup India (2016). China's Torch programme (1988) gave it a 28-year head start in building the infrastructure for innovation commercialisation. Persistent · Today R&D at 0.65% of GDP India's GERD has barely moved from 0.6–0.7% for 20+ years while China reached 2.58%. India's private sector funds only 37% of R&D (vs China's 76%). The government is simultaneously funder and user — breaking the industry-academia innovation loop that drives private R&D growth. Persistent · Today Brain drain without retention \~36% of top JEE rankers migrate abroad. 37% of US S&E doctorates go to foreign students (largely Chinese and Indian), most staying in the US. India trains talent for the world without building the domestic research environments, salaries, and infrastructure to retain them. Structural · Deep The services-manufacturing duality India's growth model generates GDP but insufficient employment. 12 million enter the workforce annually; formal manufacturing absorbs a fraction. The informal economy (80%+ of workers) is a symptom of the missing manufacturing base. Reversing this requires industrial policy, infrastructure, and vocational training working together. --- ### What India can still do — and where it has genuine strengths Strength · Use it English + Digital + Young demographics The demographic dividend window is open - India's median age is 28; China's is 39 and rising. The manufacturing workforce advantage belongs to India now, not China. - English-language proficiency is a structural advantage in the global knowledge economy that China does not have and cannot easily replicate. - India's digital public infrastructure (UPI, Aadhaar, ONDC) shows that state-led platform-building at scale is possible in a democracy. Strength · Build on it Pharma, Space, IT Proven sectors with scalable templates - India supplies 20% of global generic medicines — manufacturing at scale, with IP protection, quality standards, and global markets. A template for other sectors. - ISRO's cost efficiency (Chandrayaan at \<$100M vs NASA equivalents at $1B+) shows what focused state institutions can achieve. - These sectors demonstrate that India *can* build world-class manufacturing when the policy, talent, and regulatory environment align. Priority · Most urgent NEP 2020 + Vocational scaling Fix the middle of the pyramid - National Education Policy 2020 emphasizes vocational integration from Class 6 onward. Implementation is the test — not policy design. - Target: 25–30% of upper secondary students in vocational tracks (from 2% today). Requires industry partnerships, not just government ITIs. - The Germany Dual System model — apprenticeships co-funded and co-designed by firms — is the benchmark. Priority · Systemic R&D at 2% of GDP The private sector must lead - India's long-term goal of 2% GERD requires private sector R&D to grow from 37% to 60%+ of total. This requires tax incentives, procurement policies, and deep-tech park infrastructure. - PLI schemes are a start — but linking them to R&D investment requirements (as South Korea's chaebol model did) would compound the effect. - More IISERs, more research-intensive universities, more post-doctoral opportunities with competitive salaries to retain PhDs in India. **The honest synthesis:** India's problem is not that it built IITs. It is that it didn't build the vocational layer, the incubation infrastructure, the industrial policy, and the primary education quality simultaneously. The IITs were a good strategic bet that paid off in IT and services. The failure was the missing middle — tens of millions of technicians, operators, and skilled workers that a manufacturing economy requires. That pipeline can still be built.
